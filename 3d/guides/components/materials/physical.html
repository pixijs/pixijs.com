Physical extensions

Beyond base colour, metalness and roughness, Material3D takes the glTF material extensions in the table below. A glTF model that uses one of them loads with the matching option set.

Each of these features is an option group. Passing the group turns the feature on, even when the group is empty, so clearcoat: {} adds a full-strength, mirror-smooth coat. The material then has a property of the same name, such as material.clearcoat, for changing the group's values. That property is undefined on a material built without the group.

const paint = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0x8c1c13, roughness: 0.5, clearcoat: {} });

paint.clearcoat!.roughness = 0.1;

Which groups a material has is fixed when you create it, as Materials explains.

GroupglTF extensionWhat it adds
clearcoatKHR_materials_clearcoatA clear lacquer layer with its own highlight
sheenKHR_materials_sheenA soft glow at the edges, as on cloth
specularKHR_materials_specularThe strength and tint of a non-metal's reflection
iridescenceKHR_materials_iridescenceColour that shifts with the viewing angle
transmissionKHR_materials_transmission, KHR_materials_volumeGlass and other surfaces you see through
anisotropyKHR_materials_anisotropyHighlights stretched in one direction

ior, from KHR_materials_ior, is a plain option rather than a group. PBR materials covers the emissive group.

Clearcoat​

A clearcoat is a clear layer over the surface, as on car paint or varnished wood. The coat has its own roughness and highlight and reflects the view's environment, so rough paint can show a sharp reflection on top.

const carPaint = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0x8c1c13, roughness: 0.5, clearcoat: { roughness: 0.03 } });

The coat ignores the material's normalTexture. For bumps in the coat, such as the orange peel of sprayed lacquer, give it its own normalTexture when you create the material.

Clearcoat puts a coated sphere beside a bare one in the same paint.

Sheen​

Sheen is the soft glow on velvet, satin and other cloth. It gathers at the edges of a shape, where you see the surface at a grazing angle.

const velvet = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0x3b0a45, roughness: 0.9, sheen: { color: 0xd8a0ff, roughness: 0.8 } });

roughness sets how far the glow spreads. A low value keeps it to a thin rim, as on satin, and a high one spreads it across the surface, as on velvet.

Sheen shows the PixiJS logo in velvet. In front of it, two velvet spheres differ only in the sheen. One has a pale rim all round its outline, and the other darkens to its edge.

Specular​

The specular group sets the strength and tint of a non-metal's reflection. factor runs from 0, for no reflection, to 1, for the reflection the material has without the group. color tints the reflection. The group has no effect on metals, which reflect with their base colour.

const dimmed = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0x111114, roughness: 0.05, specular: { factor: 0.25 } });
const warm = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0x111114, roughness: 0.05, specular: { color: 0xff6030 } });

Specular and IOR changes the group's factor and color on glossy black plastic, where the reflection is all that shows.

Index of refraction​

ior, the index of refraction, is how strongly a material bends light that passes into it. It also sets how much a non-metal reflects when you look at it head-on. The default, 1.5, suits glass and most plastics. Water is 1.33 and reflects less; diamond is about 2.4 and reflects more. It doesn't change how a metal reflects.

const water = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0xd8f0ff, roughness: 0.05, ior: 1.33, transmission: { thickness: 1 } });

In Specular and IOR, five spheres of glossy black plastic run from an ior of 1, which reflects nothing head-on, to diamond's 2.42.

Iridescence​

Iridescence is a thin film on the surface, as on a soap bubble, an oil slick or a beetle's shell. The film colours the surface's reflections and highlights, and the colour shifts with the angle you see it from.

const beetle = new Material3D({
  baseColor: 0x112211,
  metallic: 1,
  roughness: 0.2,
  iridescence: { thicknessMaximum: 500 },
});

The film's thickness, in nanometres, decides its colours. Without a texture, thicknessMaximum sets it.

Transmission and volume​

The transmission group lets light through the surface, for glass, water, gems and clear plastic. alphaMode: 'blend' fades a surface. Transmission instead bends what's behind the surface by the material's ior, blurs it on rough glass and tints it with the base colour.

const glass = new Material3D({ roughness: 0.05, transmission: { thickness: 0.5 } });

On WebGL2, glass can also need the application's useBackBuffer option. How glass draws says when.

thickness is how far light travels inside the object, in the mesh's own units, so it scales with the mesh. At 0, the default, the surface is a thin wall, such as a window pane, and what's behind it shows through without bending. Give a solid object a thickness close to its own size. The volume can also tint the light that passes through it, as wine does. Like bending, the tint needs a thickness above 0:

const wine = new Material3D({
  roughness: 0,
  transmission: { thickness: 1, attenuationDistance: 1, attenuationColor: 0x7a0f1e },
});

Glass and liquids shows a fence of bars through a glass logo, which bends them, and through a bottle of liquid, which tints them.

How glass draws​

The view draws the opaque meshes, copies the picture so far, then draws the glass over it. What's in the copy decides what you see through glass. The copy holds:

  • the background
  • the opaque meshes
  • what 'afterOpaque' items in view3d.passes draw into the scene
  • in a view drawn straight to the screen, the 2D content drawn before the view, as Glass over 2D shows

The copy doesn't hold other glass, so glass behind glass doesn't show through it.

The copy doesn't hold blended meshes either, and sprites and particles are blended by default. Glass also hides blended meshes where they're behind it. To see sprites or particles through glass, give them an alphaMode of 'mask', which draws them hard-edged with the opaque meshes. Building the glass with alphaMode: 'blend' doesn't help, because the sprites and particles then draw on top of the glass, unbent.

A Mesh3D whose material has transmission casts no shadow, even with castShadow on.

In composite mode, such as while a post effect is on, the view draws into a texture of its own, so its glass shows only the view's own 3D. Put what should show through the glass in the scene. For 2D content, draw it onto a 3D surface, as Rendering 2D into 3D shows.

warning

On WebGL2, glass in a view drawn straight onto an opaque canvas, PixiJS's default, shows nothing behind it and looks black apart from its reflections. Start the application with useBackBuffer: true to fix it. WebGPU doesn't need the option.

await app.init({ preference: 'webgpu', useBackBuffer: true });

On WebGL2 the option costs an extra full-screen draw every frame. It also breaks the lighting of doubleSided materials. In an app that has them, leave it off and give the view composite: true instead, since glass in composite mode works without it.

Anisotropy​

Anisotropy stretches highlights along one direction, as on brushed metal, a vinyl record or hair.

const brushed = new Material3D({ baseColor: 0xb0b0b0, metallic: 1, roughness: 0.4, anisotropy: { strength: 0.8 } });

The stretch runs along the surface's tangent, a direction that lies in the surface. The tangent comes from the geometry's tangents when it has them, and otherwise from its UVs, the texture coordinates. rotation turns the stretch. For a direction that varies across the surface, such as the circles on a record, give the group a texture when you create the material.

What each feature costs​

Each group adds work to every pixel the material covers. The cost comes with the group, not its values. A clearcoat with a factor of 0 looks the same as no coat and costs as much as a full one. A group's texture slots, listed in Material3DTextures, count toward the texture limit that Materials describes, whether or not you fill them.

GroupExtra work per pixel
specularA few instructions
iridescenceA thin-film colour calculation
clearcoatA second highlight for each light, and a second reflection
sheenA sheen highlight for each light, and a reflection
anisotropyA second highlight calculation for each light
transmissionA read of the copy of the picture

Glass also costs the copy itself, every frame that glass is in the scene, as How a frame renders explains.
